Fluency and coherence

The first Speaking criterion is about keeping going and making sense: talking at a natural pace without too much effort, and linking what you say into connected speech.

What the official criteria say

The "IELTS Speaking Key Assessment Criteria" document, read on 12 November 2026, describes fluency and coherence as the ability to talk with normal levels of continuity, rate and effort, and to link ideas and language together. In our words, the indicators are:

  • Speech rate. Not too slow. Very slow speech makes it hard for a listener to hold the ideas together.
  • Continuity. The flow should not be broken too often by false starts, going back, repeating words for no reason, or long pauses while you search for a word.
  • Logical order of what you say.
  • Clear marking of stages in a description or argument, using pauses and spoken linking words.
  • Relevance to the question.
  • Cohesive devices: connectors, pronouns and conjunctions.

Fluent does not mean fast

Many candidates try to speak quickly and then stumble. Aim for a steady, natural pace, the speed at which you would explain something to a colleague. Short pauses to think are normal in any language. What counts against you is the pause in the middle of a phrase while you hunt for a word, and the habit of restarting sentences.

Thinking time without silence

Native speakers fill thinking time with small phrases. A few, used naturally, are useful:

  • "That's an interesting question."
  • "Let me think about that for a moment."
  • "I've never really thought about it, but I suppose ..."
  • "It depends, really."

Do not learn a long list and use one before every answer. That sounds memorised.

Extending an answer

One-word answers give the examiner nothing to assess. A simple pattern for Part 1: answer, reason, example.

Question: Do you like cooking?

Short: "Yes, I do."

Extended: "Yes, I do, mainly because it helps me relax after work. On Sundays I usually make something that takes time, like biryani, and the whole family eats together."

Linking in speech

Spoken linking words are simpler than written ones: and, but, so, because, also, then, actually, for example, on the other hand. "Moreover" and "furthermore" belong in essays and sound stiff in conversation.

Self-correction

Correcting a slip once, quickly, is fine: "He go, he goes to work by bus." Stopping to correct every small error breaks the flow and costs more than the error did.

Exercises

  1. One minute, no stopping. Pick an everyday topic, your street, your phone, your breakfast, and talk for one minute without stopping. If you cannot find a word, explain it another way and keep going.
  2. Record and count. Record a two-minute answer. Listen and count the long pauses and restarts. Do the same topic again and try to halve them.
  3. Shadowing. Play a short clip of clear spoken English, and speak along with it a moment behind, copying pace and rhythm.
  4. Daily English. Speak English for ten minutes every day with a friend or to yourself. Fluency comes from quantity.
